Describing a scatterplot

A scatterplot shows the association between two numerical variables, with the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis. Describe it in three words, always in this order.

FeatureOptionsWhat to look for
Directionpositive / negative / no associationDoes the response tend to increase or decrease as the explanatory variable increases?
Formlinear / non-linearDo the points follow a straight line or a curve?
Strengthstrong / moderate / weakHow tightly do the points cluster around the pattern?

“There is a strong, positive, linear association between hours of study and test score.” Name both variables. If there is an outlier, mention it too. If a question asks only for one feature (for example, the form), give just that.

Pearson's correlation coefficient, r

The correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the strength and direction of a linear association, from \(-1\) (perfect negative) through 0 (no linear association) to \(+1\) (perfect positive). Your CAS gives \(r\) with the regression output.

Definition (calculated with technology)
\[ r = \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum \left(\frac{x - \bar x}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y - \bar y}{s_y}\right) \]

The average product of the z-scores: points in the top-right and bottom-left make \(r\) positive.

Value of r (a common guide)Strength of linear association
\(0.75 \le |r| \le 1\)strong
\(0.5 \le |r| < 0.75\)moderate
\(0.25 \le |r| < 0.5\)weak
\(|r| < 0.25\)no (linear) association

\(r\) is only meaningful when (1) both variables are numerical, (2) the association is linear, and (3) there are no clear outliers. A curved scatterplot can have a perfect relationship and \(r \approx 0\); one outlier can turn a weak \(r\) into a strong one.

A steep line doesn't mean a large \(r\). \(r\) measures how close the points are to a line, not how steep it is. Swapping the axes doesn't change \(r\).

For 15 used cars, the correlation between age (years) and price (\$) is \(r = -0.68\). Describe the association, assuming the scatterplot is linear with no outliers.

The sign of \(r\) gives the direction; its size, compared with the guide, gives the strength.

Negative sign: price tends to decrease as age increases. \(0.5 \le 0.68 < 0.75\): moderate.

A moderate, negative, linear association: older cars tend to be cheaper.

Association is not causation

A strong association, even \(r = 0.99\), never proves that one variable causes the other. There are other possible explanations:

ExplanationWhat happensExample
Common responseA third variable drives bothIce-cream sales and drownings both rise with hot weather
Confounding variableAnother variable is mixed in with the explanatory one, so their effects can't be separatedPeople who drink coffee also smoke more, so a link between coffee and heart disease might be due to smoking
CoincidenceThe association is a chance patternThe number of films an actor made each year and a country's cheese consumption
Reverse directionThe response actually drives the “explanatory” variableCities with more police have more crime: more crime leads to hiring more police

In an observational study you record what happens without intervening, so other variables can't be controlled. In an experiment you deliberately change the explanatory variable (ideally with random allocation and a control group) while keeping everything else the same. Only a well-designed experiment can establish cause and effect.

“No. Although there is a strong positive association, this does not mean that X causes Y. A possible explanation is a third variable, such as Z, that affects both.” Always name a plausible third variable when asked for one.

Practice

A scatterplot shows points closely following a straight line sloping down from left to right. The best estimate of r is

  1. 0.9
  2. −0.9
  3. −0.3
  4. 0

Sloping down: negative. Close to a line: strong.

Using the common guide, r = 0.42 indicates

  1. a strong positive linear association
  2. a moderate positive linear association
  3. no association
  4. a weak positive linear association

\(0.25 \le 0.42 < 0.5\): weak.

The points on a scatterplot lie exactly on a U-shaped curve. Which statement is true?

  1. r = 1 because the relationship is perfect
  2. r = −1
  3. r may be close to 0, so it is not a suitable measure here
  4. r cannot be calculated for this data

\(r\) only measures linear association. A symmetric U-shape gives \(r\) near 0 even though the relationship is perfect.

Across Australian towns, the number of doctors is strongly positively associated with the number of deaths each year. The most likely explanation is

  1. both depend on the population of the town
  2. doctors cause deaths
  3. deaths cause more doctors to be hired
  4. it is a coincidence

Common response: larger towns have more doctors and more deaths.

For 20 days, the correlation between daily maximum temperature and the number of drinks sold at a café is \(r = 0.86\). Describe the association, stating any assumption you make.

A strong, positive association: on hotter days more drinks tend to be sold. [1]

Calling it linear assumes the scatterplot shows a linear pattern with no outliers. [1]

A newspaper says: “Students who eat breakfast get better marks, so eating breakfast improves your marks.” Explain why this conclusion is not justified, and name a possible confounding variable.

The data come from observation, so an association between breakfast and marks does not show cause and effect. [1]

A possible confounding variable is family routine or income (for example, students with more structured home lives may both eat breakfast and study more). [1]

Summary

  • Direction (positive/negative), form (linear/non-linear), strength (strong/moderate/weak), plus any outliers.
  • The strength and direction of a linear association, from −1 to 1. Strong ≥ 0.75, moderate 0.5–0.75, weak 0.25–0.5, none below 0.25 (in absolute value).
  • Both variables numerical, the association linear, and no clear outliers.
  • Possible common response, confounding variable, coincidence or reverse direction. Only a controlled experiment establishes causation.
