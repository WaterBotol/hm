Culling and restocking: Sₙ₊₁ = TSₙ + B

A plain transition matrix keeps the total fixed. To model a population where some are added (restocking) or removed (culling) every period, add a column matrix \(B\) after the transition.

With culling and restocking (on the formula sheet)
\[ S_0 = \text{initial state}, \qquad S_{n+1} = T S_n + B \]

Positive elements of \(B\) are added each period; negative elements are removed. The order matters: transition first, then add \(B\).

Two connected ponds, P and Q, hold 400 and 600 fish. Each month 10% of P's fish move to Q and 20% of Q's move to P. After the move, 50 fish are added to P and 30 are caught from Q. (a) Write \(T\) and \(B\). (b) Find the numbers in each pond after 2 months.

Build \(T\) as usual (columns = from). \(B\) holds +50 for P and −30 for Q. Apply the rule one step at a time: \(T^nS_0\) does not work once \(B\) is involved.

(a) \(T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}\), \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ -30 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 400 \\ 600 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 360 + 120 \\ 40 + 480 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ -30 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 530 \\ 490 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(S_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 477 + 98 \\ 53 + 392 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ -30 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 625 \\ 415 \end{bmatrix}\).

After 2 months: 625 in P and 415 in Q. The total grows by \(50 - 30 = 20\) fish every month (1000, 1020, 1040).

\(S_n = T^nS_0\) only works without \(B\). With culling or restocking, use the recurrence step by step (CAS: store \(S\), then repeat \(T \times S + B \to S\)). Also check whether a question culls or adds before or after the transition; the formula sheet version is after.

Leslie matrices

A Leslie matrix \(L\) models a population (usually females only) split into age groups of equal width. Each period, every age group has babies and some of each group survive into the next group.

Leslie matrix for three age groups (recurrence on the formula sheet)
\[ L = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ s_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & s_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad S_0 = \text{initial state}, \qquad S_{n+1} = L S_n \]

First row: birth rates \(b_i\) (average female offspring per female in each age group). Below the leading diagonal: survival rates \(s_i\) (proportion of group \(i\) that survives into group \(i + 1\)). Every other element is 0.

Unlike a transition matrix, a Leslie matrix creates new members (births) and loses some (deaths), so the total changes. The last group has no survival rate out of it: those animals don't survive to the next period.

Females of a small mammal are in three age groups: 0–1, 1–2 and 2–3 years. Each year, a female aged 1–2 has 1.6 female offspring on average and one aged 2–3 has 0.8; none are born to the youngest group. Half of the 0–1 group survive to 1–2, and 60% of the 1–2 group survive to 2–3. Start with 200, 100 and 50 females. Find the population after 1 and 2 years.

Birth rates go in row 1; survival rates sit just below the leading diagonal.

\(L = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1.6 & 0.8 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 200 \\ 100 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.6(100) + 0.8(50) \\ 0.5(200) \\ 0.6(100) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 200 \\ 100 \\ 60 \end{bmatrix}\). \(S_2 = L S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 208 \\ 100 \\ 60 \end{bmatrix}\).

After 1 year: 200, 100, 60 (total 360). After 2 years: 208, 100, 60 (total 368).

To decide whether a Leslie population is growing in the long run, compare totals at two large \(n\) values (e.g. \(n = 50\) and \(n = 51\)). A ratio above 1 means growth. Round final answers to whole animals only when the question asks, and keep unrounded values for the next step.

Practice

In the Leslie matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 1.4 & 0.5 \\ 0.7 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.4 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), the proportion of the second age group that survives into the third is

  1. 1.4
  2. 0.7
  3. 0.4
  4. 0.5

Survival from group 2 to group 3 is in row 3, column 2.

With \(T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}\), \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ -30 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(S_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 625 \\ 415 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_3\) is

  1. \(\begin{bmatrix} 695.5 \\ 364.5 \end{bmatrix}\)
  2. \(\begin{bmatrix} 645.5 \\ 394.5 \end{bmatrix}\)
  3. \(\begin{bmatrix} 625 \\ 415 \end{bmatrix}\)
  4. \(\begin{bmatrix} 745.5 \\ 334.5 \end{bmatrix}\)

\(TS_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 645.5 \\ 394.5 \end{bmatrix}\), then add \(B\). B forgets to add \(B\).

For \(S_{n+1} = TS_n + B\), which statement is true?

  1. \(S_3 = T^3S_0 + 3B\)
  2. \(S_3 = T^3(S_0 + B)\)
  3. \(S_3 = T^3S_0 + B\)
  4. \(S_3\) must be found by applying the rule three times

Each added \(B\) is itself transitioned in later steps, so none of the shortcuts work. Apply the rule step by step.

A population has Leslie matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0.4 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\). In the long run, this population

  1. grows by 20% every period
  2. shrinks, because each young female produces on average \(0.4 \times 2 = 0.8\) daughters
  3. stays the same size
  4. doubles every period

A newborn survives with probability 0.4 and then has 2 daughters: 0.8 per female over two periods, below replacement. Check on CAS: totals fall over time.

Using the mammal Leslie matrix and \(S_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 208 \\ 100 \\ 60 \end{bmatrix}\), find \(S_3\). How many females in the 0–1 group are newborns from the 2–3 group?

\(S_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1.6(100) + 0.8(60) \\ 0.5(208) \\ 0.6(100) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 208 \\ 104 \\ 60 \end{bmatrix}\). [1]

\(0.8 \times 60 = 48\). [1]

Each period, 20 young females are added after breeding, so \(S_{n+1} = LS_n + B\). Start with 100 in the young group and 200 in the older group. (a) Write \(B\). (b) Find the numbers after two periods.

Summary

  • \(S_{n+1} = TS_n + B\). Positive elements are added (restocked); negative elements are removed (culled).
  • Each period's \(B\) is transitioned in later periods, so you must apply the rule step by step.
  • Birth rates are in the first row. Survival rates are just below the leading diagonal (row \(i + 1\), column \(i\)).
  • Compare totals at two large \(n\) values: a ratio above 1 means growth, below 1 means decline. The age-group proportions also settle.
