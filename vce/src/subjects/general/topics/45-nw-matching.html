Bipartite graphs

A bipartite graph has two groups of vertices (say people and jobs) with edges only between the groups. Matching problems ask you to pair each person with one job.

Each student must run exactly one club. Allocate them.

Start with any vertex that has only one edge: its choice is forced.

Ben can only do Art. Then Ann must do Band, leaving Choir for Cat.

Ben–Art, Ann–Band, Cat–Choir.

The Hungarian algorithm

When each pairing has a cost (or time), the Hungarian algorithm finds the allocation with the minimum total. Work on the cost matrix: rows are people, columns are tasks.

Hungarian algorithm
  1. Row reduce: subtract the smallest element of each row from that row.
  2. Column reduce: subtract the smallest element of each column from that column.
  3. Cover all the zeros with the fewest possible horizontal and vertical lines. If the number of lines equals the number of rows, go to step 5.
  4. Adjust: find the smallest uncovered element. Subtract it from every uncovered element and add it to every element covered twice (where lines cross). Go back to step 3.
  5. Allocate using the zeros (one per row and column), then add up the original costs.

Four tradespeople can each do one job. Times (hours):

Find the allocation with the minimum total time.

Reduce rows, then columns, then cover and adjust until 4 lines are needed.

Row minimums 7, 7, 8, 6, then column minimums 0, 0, 2, 0: \(\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 7 & 14 & 20 \\ 15 & 19 & 14 & 7 \\ 8 & 18 & 10 & 15 \\ 9 & 20 & 18 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 7 & 13 \\ 8 & 12 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 & 2 & 7 \\ 3 & 14 & 12 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \to \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 5 & 13 \\ 8 & 12 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 & 0 & 7 \\ 3 & 14 & 10 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)

The zeros are covered by 3 lines (rows Ari and Cy, column Plumb). That's fewer than 4, so adjust: the smallest uncovered element is 3. Subtract 3 from uncovered elements; add 3 where lines cross: \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 5 & 16 \\ 5 & 9 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 & 0 & 10 \\ 0 & 11 & 7 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)

Now 4 lines are needed. Allocate: Ari's only zero is Tile; Bo's only zero is Plumb; Di then gets Paint; Cy gets Wire.

Ari–Tile, Bo–Plumb, Cy–Wire, Di–Paint: \(7 + 7 + 10 + 9 = \) 33 hours.

The reduced matrix only tells you which pairs to use. The total comes from the original table. A total of 0 is the classic giveaway that someone added the reduced matrix.

The Hungarian algorithm minimises. To maximise (profit, score), first subtract every element from the largest element in the table, then run the algorithm as usual, and add up the original values for the chosen pairs.

If a row has two zeros that both lead to complete allocations, there are several optimal allocations with the same total. Questions often ask for “all possible” allocations: test each choice.

Practice

After row reduction, the first row of \(\begin{bmatrix} 9 & 4 & 7 \\ \cdots \end{bmatrix}\) becomes

  1. 0, 5, 2
  2. 9, 4, 7
  3. 5, 0, 3
  4. 2, 0, 3

Subtract the row's smallest element, 4: \(5, 0, 3\).

In a 5 × 5 Hungarian problem, the zeros can be covered by 4 lines. The next step is to

  1. adjust using the smallest uncovered element
  2. allocate using the zeros
  3. row reduce again
  4. add up the original costs

4 lines is fewer than 5, so an allocation isn't possible yet.

During the adjustment step, an element covered by two lines

  1. stays the same
  2. becomes 0
  3. has the smallest uncovered element subtracted
  4. has the smallest uncovered element added

Uncovered elements drop by the smallest uncovered value, elements covered once stay, and elements covered twice go up by it.

A manager wants to maximise total sales by allocating staff to stores. Before using the Hungarian algorithm, the manager should

  1. multiply every element by −1 and add 100
  2. subtract every element from the largest element
  3. column reduce first instead of row reduce
  4. divide every element by the largest element

“Largest minus element” turns the biggest sales into the smallest costs, without negatives.

Use the Hungarian algorithm to allocate three machines (rows P, Q, R) to three tasks (columns 1, 2, 3) with costs \(\begin{bmatrix} 11 & 8 & 11 \\ 5 & 7 & 12 \\ 2 & 6 & 11 \end{bmatrix}\) ($100s), and find the minimum cost.

2 lines (row P, column 1) cover the zeros. Smallest uncovered is 2: \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), which needs 3 lines. [1]

R–1 (only zero), Q–2, P–3: \(2 + 7 + 11 = 20\), so $2000. [1]

Summary

  • Two groups of vertices with edges only between groups. Start with any vertex that has only one possible match.
  • Row reduce; column reduce; cover zeros with fewest lines; if fewer lines than rows, adjust (subtract smallest uncovered from uncovered, add to intersections) and repeat; allocate.
  • The original table, for the allocated pairs.
  • Subtract every element from the largest element first, then minimise as usual.
