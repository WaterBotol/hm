Order and elements

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. A matrix with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns has order \(m \times n\) (rows first). The element in row \(i\), column \(j\) is \(a_{ij}\).

A 2 × 3 matrix
\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad a_{12} = -1, \quad a_{23} = 4 \]

Order is rows × columns, and \(a_{ij}\) is row \(i\), column \(j\). A matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns is 3 × 2, not 2 × 3. Remember “RC”: rows, then columns.

Types of matrices

Rowone row\(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\)
Columnone column\(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}\)

Diagonalsquare; every element off the leading diagonal is 0\(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\)
Identity, \(I\)diagonal with 1s on the leading diagonal\(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
Symmetricsquare; equal to its transpose (\(a_{ij} = a_{ji}\))\(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 7 \\ 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\)
Triangularsquare; all zeros below (upper triangular) or above (lower) the leading diagonal\(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)
Zero, \(O\)every element is 0\(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\)
Binaryevery element is 0 or 1\(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\)

The transpose \(A^T\) swaps rows and columns: row 1 becomes column 1, and so on. A 2 × 3 matrix has a 3 × 2 transpose.

Adding, subtracting and scalar multiples

Add or subtract matrices of the same order element by element. To multiply by a scalar \(k\), multiply every element by \(k\).

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 12 \\ 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Multiplying matrices

The product \(AB\) is defined only when the number of columns of A equals the number of rows of B. The result has the rows of \(A\) and the columns of \(B\).

When a product is defined
\[ \underset{m \times \color{#007aff}{n}}{A} \;\; \underset{\color{#007aff}{n} \times p}{B} = \underset{m \times p}{AB} \]

Each element of \(AB\) is a row of \(A\) times a column of \(B\): multiply matching entries and add.

Find \(AB\) for \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(A\) is 2 × 3 and \(B\) is 3 × 2: the inner numbers match, so \(AB\) is 2 × 2. Element (row \(i\), column \(j\)) = row \(i\) of \(A\) · column \(j\) of \(B\).

Row 1: \(1(2) + 2(0) + 0(1) = 2\) and \(1(1) + 2(5) + 0(-2) = 11\).

Row 2: \(3(2) - 1(0) + 4(1) = 10\) and \(3(1) - 1(5) + 4(-2) = -10\).

\(AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 11 \\ 10 & -10 \end{bmatrix}\).

In general \(AB \ne BA\), and one of them may not even be defined. Check the orders every time. Powers like \(A^2 = AA\) only exist for square matrices. For any matrix, \(AI = IA = A\).

Matrices from rules and tables

Write the 2 × 3 matrix \(M\) with \(m_{ij} = 2i - j\).

Substitute each row number \(i\) and column number \(j\).

Row 1: \(2 - 1, 2 - 2, 2 - 3 = 1, 0, -1\). Row 2: \(4 - 1, 4 - 2, 4 - 3 = 3, 2, 1\).

\(M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Two stores sold pies, rolls and drinks. Store 1 sold 12, 8 and 5; store 2 sold 6, 10 and 9. Prices are \$3, \$4.50 and \$6. Use matrix multiplication to find each store's revenue.

A 2 × 3 sales matrix times a 3 × 1 price matrix gives a 2 × 1 revenue matrix.

\(\begin{bmatrix} 12 & 8 & 5 \\ 6 & 10 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4.5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 36 + 36 + 30 \\ 18 + 45 + 54 \end{bmatrix}\)

Store 1: \$102; store 2: \$117.

Enter matrices with the matrix template and multiply, add or raise them to powers directly. In the exam, write the matrix expression you calculated (e.g. \(SP\)) and its order, then the answer.

  2. 0

  4. 1

\(A\) is 2 × 3 and \(B\) is 2 × 3. Which of these is defined?

  1. \(AB\)
  2. \(A^2\)
  3. \(BA\)
  4. \(A^TB\)

\(A^T\) is 3 × 2 and \(B\) is 2 × 3: inner numbers match, giving 3 × 3. The others don't match.

  1. \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
  2. \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)
  3. \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)

Every diagonal matrix is symmetric; B and D are symmetric but not diagonal.

Summary

  • Rows × columns; \(a_{ij}\) is the element in row \(i\), column \(j\).
  • Symmetric: equals its transpose. Diagonal: zeros off the leading diagonal. Triangular: zeros below (or above) the diagonal. Binary: only 0s and 1s. Identity: 1s on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere.
  • When columns of A = rows of B; AB has the rows of A and the columns of B.
  • No, order matters. Only square matrices have powers.
