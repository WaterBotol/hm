Categorical and numerical data

Every statistical question starts with the variable. Its type decides which displays and summaries you are allowed to use.

TypeSub-typeWhat it meansExamples
Categorical
(groups or labels)		NominalCategories with no natural ordereye colour, sport played, postcode, payment method
OrdinalCategories with a natural ordershirt size (S, M, L), satisfaction (poor → excellent), year level
Numerical
(quantities)		DiscreteCounts: only certain valuesnumber of siblings, goals scored, cars in a car park
ContinuousMeasurements: any value in a rangeheight, time, mass, temperature

A variable recorded with numbers is not automatically numerical. Postcodes, phone numbers and jersey numbers are nominal: averaging them is meaningless. A rating such as “1 = strongly disagree … 5 = strongly agree” is ordinal. Ask: does arithmetic on these numbers make sense?

Age in years (17, 18, 19) is numerical. Age group (“under 18”, “18–25”, “over 25”) is ordinal categorical. Read how the variable was actually recorded.

Displaying categorical data

A frequency table lists each category and its count. A percentage frequency is the count divided by the total, times 100. Display one categorical variable with a bar chart (bars separated by gaps) or a single percentage segmented bar chart (one bar split into segments that add to 100%).

Percentage frequency
\[ \text{percentage} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}} \times 100\% \]

The mode (modal category) is the category with the highest frequency. For categorical data, describe the distribution by naming the most and least common categories and giving their percentages.

Displaying numerical data

Dot plots (one dot per value) suit small discrete data sets. Stem plots keep every data value while showing shape: the stem is the leading digit(s), each leaf one trailing digit, and a key is essential. Split stems (e.g. leaves 0–4 and 5–9 on separate rows) stretch out a crowded plot. Histograms group values into intervals (bins) with no gaps between bars, and suit large or continuous data sets.

DisplayBest forKeeps raw values?
Dot plotSmall discrete data setsYes
Stem plotSmall to medium data sets (up to about 50 values)Yes
HistogramLarge data sets, continuous dataNo (only counts per interval)

A bar from 20 to 25 holds values with \(20 \le x < 25\). A value of exactly 25 belongs in the next bar. When a question asks how many values are “less than 25”, add the bars up to and including the 20–25 bar.

Describing a distribution

For a numerical variable, describe shape, centre, spread and outliers.

  • Shape: symmetric, positively skewed (tail to the right, towards higher values) or negatively skewed (tail to the left). Mention if it has more than one peak.
  • Centre: the median (or the mean for symmetric data).
  • Spread: the range or IQR (or the standard deviation for symmetric data).
  • Outliers: values that sit well apart from the rest. Check them with the fence rule on the boxplots page.

“The distribution of travel times is positively skewed with a median of 22 minutes and an IQR of 15 minutes. There are two possible outliers at 75 and 82 minutes.” Every number has a unit and the variable is named.

Log (base 10) scales

When data range over several orders of magnitude (e.g. animal body masses from grams to tonnes, city populations), a normal histogram squashes almost everything into the first bar. Plotting \(\log_{10}(x)\) spreads the data out. Each step of 1 on a log scale is a multiplication by 10.

Converting to and from a log scale
\[ \log_{10}(x) = k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = 10^{k} \]

A log value of 3 means \(10^3 = 1000\). A log value of 2.5 means \(10^{2.5} \approx 316\).

A histogram shows \(\log_{10}(\text{population})\) for 50 towns. One bar covers log values from 3.5 to 4.0 and contains 9 towns. What range of populations does this bar represent, and what percentage of the towns is it?

Undo the log on each end of the interval: \(x = 10^{k}\). The percentage uses the total number of towns.

\(10^{3.5} \approx 3162\) and \(10^{4} = 10\,000\).

\(\frac{9}{50} \times 100\% = 18\%\).

Populations from about 3162 up to (but not including) 10 000; 18% of the towns.

A stem plot (key: 3 | 4 = 34 minutes) shows the times 19 students took to finish a quiz. Row by row the leaves are: 1 | 5 8 9, 2 | 0 2 4 4 7, 3 | 1 3 3 5 6 8, 4 | 0 2 6, 5 | 1 9. Find the median and describe the shape.

With \(n\) values, the median is the \(\frac{n + 1}{2}\)th value in order. Stem plots are already ordered.

\(\frac{19 + 1}{2} = 10\): the 10th value. Counting: 3 values in the 10s, 5 in the 20s (8 so far), then 31, 33. The 10th value is 33.

Median = 33 minutes. The distribution is roughly symmetric (the 30s row is the longest, with similar tails either side) with no obvious outliers.

Practice

Which variable is ordinal?

  1. Number of pets owned
  2. Postcode
  3. Level of agreement (low, medium, high)
  4. Body temperature in °C

Low/medium/high are categories with a natural order. Postcode is nominal; the other two are numerical.

A histogram's tail stretches out to the right towards high values. The distribution is

  1. negatively skewed
  2. positively skewed
  3. symmetric
  4. bimodal

Skew is named after the tail: tail to the right (positive end) = positively skewed.

On a histogram of \(\log_{10}(\text{mass in grams})\), an animal plotted at 4 is how many times heavier than one plotted at 2?

  1. 2
  2. 20
  3. 2 times as heavy plus 100 g
  4. 100

\(10^4 \div 10^2 = 100\). Two steps on a log scale = ×10 × 10.

Shoe sizes are recorded for 200 students as 6, 6.5, 7, …, 12. The most suitable description is

  1. numerical discrete
  2. numerical continuous
  3. categorical nominal
  4. categorical ordinal only, so no histogram is possible

Shoe sizes are quantities that can only take certain values (half-size steps), so they are numerical discrete. Foot length would be continuous.

In a survey of 80 people, 28 preferred tea, 36 preferred coffee and 16 preferred neither. Find the percentage that preferred coffee and name the modal category.

\(\frac{36}{80} \times 100\% = 45\%\). [1]

Modal category: coffee. [1]

A histogram of \(\log_{10}(\text{annual income in dollars})\) has an interval from 4.6 to 4.8. Write the incomes this interval represents, to the nearest dollar.

\(10^{4.6} \approx 39\,811\). [1]

\(10^{4.8} \approx 63\,096\): incomes from about \$39 811 to \$63 096. [1]

Summary

  • Categorical: nominal (no order) or ordinal (ordered). Numerical: discrete (counts) or continuous (measurements).
  • Categorical: frequency tables, bar charts, segmented bar charts. Numerical: dot plots, stem plots, histograms.
  • Shape, centre, spread, outliers. Skew is named by the side of the tail (tail right = positive skew).
  • \(x = 10^{k}\). Each step of 1 is a factor of 10.
