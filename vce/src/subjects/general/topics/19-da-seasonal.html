What a seasonal index means

A seasonal index (SI) compares a season with the average season. An index of 1 means a typical season; above 1 means busier than average; below 1 means quieter.

A seasonal index of 1.30 for summer means summer values are typically 30% above the seasonal average. An index of 0.85 for winter means winter values are typically 15% below the average.

Seasonal indices always add to the number of seasons
\[ \sum \text{SI} = 4 \text{ (quarterly)}, \qquad 12 \text{ (monthly)}, \qquad 7 \text{ (daily, for days of the week)} \]

Use this to find a missing index: subtract the known indices from the total.

Calculating seasonal indices

  1. Find the yearly mean (the mean of the seasons in each year).
  2. Divide each value by its own year's mean.
  3. Average these ratios for each season across the years. That average is the seasonal index.

Quarterly sales were Year 1: 120, 150, 210, 160 and Year 2: 140, 170, 240, 190. Calculate the seasonal index for each quarter.

Divide each value by its yearly mean, then average the two ratios for each quarter. Check that the indices add to 4.

Yearly means: \(\frac{640}{4} = 160\) and \(\frac{740}{4} = 185\).

Year 1 ratios: 0.75, 0.9375, 1.3125, 1.0. Year 2 ratios: 0.7568, 0.9189, 1.2973, 1.0270.

Average each quarter: Q1 \(\frac{0.75 + 0.7568}{2} = 0.7534\), Q2 0.9282, Q3 1.3049, Q4 1.0135 (sum 4.0000).

SI: Q1 0.753, Q2 0.928, Q3 1.305, Q4 1.014. Quarter 3 sales are typically about 30% above average.

Deseasonalising and reseasonalising

Deseasonalised data show what the value would have been without the seasonal effect, so you can see the trend.

Seasonal index (on the formula sheet) and its rearrangements
\[ \text{seasonal index} = \frac{\text{actual figure}}{\text{deseasonalised figure}} \]\[ \text{deseasonalised} = \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{SI}} \qquad \text{actual} = \text{deseasonalised} \times \text{SI} \]

Using a Q3 seasonal index of 1.305, deseasonalise Year 2 Q3 sales of 240.

Divide the actual figure by the seasonal index.

\(\frac{240}{1.305} = 183.9\).

183.9: once the summer boost is removed, Q3 sales were close to the Year 2 average of 185.

A seasonal index of 1.25 means values are 25% above average. To deseasonalise you divide by 1.25, which is the same as multiplying by 0.8: a 20% decrease, not 25%. Similarly an SI of 0.8 needs a 25% increase (\(\frac{1}{0.8} = 1.25\)). This appears in exams almost every year.

Trend lines and forecasts

Number the time periods 1, 2, 3, … and fit a least squares line with time as the explanatory variable. If the data are seasonal, fit the line to the deseasonalised data. To forecast:

  1. Substitute the future time period into the trend line (this gives a deseasonalised forecast).
  2. Multiply by that season's index to reseasonalise.

The trend line for deseasonalised quarterly sales is \(\text{deseasonalised sales} = 150 + 4.2t\), where \(t = 1\) is Q1 of Year 1. Using SI = 0.75 for Q1, forecast actual sales in Q1 of Year 4.

Q1 of Year 4 is \(t = 13\). The line gives a deseasonalised value, so reseasonalise by multiplying by the Q1 index.

\(150 + 4.2 \times 13 = 204.6\) (deseasonalised).

\(204.6 \times 0.75 = 153.45\).

Forecast actual sales: about 153.

A forecast assumes the trend stays linear and the seasonal pattern stays the same. The further into the future you forecast, the less reliable it is (extrapolation). Structural changes, such as new competitors or a pandemic, can break the pattern completely.

Practice

The seasonal indices for three quarters are 0.85, 1.20 and 0.92. The seasonal index for the fourth quarter is

  1. 0.97
  2. 1.03
  3. 1.00
  4. 2.97

\(4 - (0.85 + 1.20 + 0.92) = 4 - 2.97 = 1.03\).

Actual sales in a quarter were 300 and the seasonal index is 1.2. The deseasonalised sales are

  1. 360
  2. 240
  3. 250
  4. 301.2

\(\frac{300}{1.2} = 250\).

The seasonal index for December is 1.60. To correct December figures for seasonality, they should be

  1. decreased by 37.5%
  2. decreased by 60%
  3. increased by 60%
  4. decreased by 1.6%

Divide by 1.6: \(\frac{1}{1.6} = 0.625\), a 37.5% decrease.

A trend line for deseasonalised monthly visitor numbers is \(\text{deseasonalised visitors} = 2400 + 35t\), where \(t = 1\) is January 2024. The seasonal index for July is 0.70. The forecast for July 2025 is closest to

  1. 2659
  2. 3065
  3. 1953
  4. 2146

July 2025 is \(t = 19\): \(2400 + 35 \times 19 = 3065\), then \(3065 \times 0.70 = 2145.5\).

A café's seasonal index for winter is 0.82. Interpret this, and find the deseasonalised value of winter takings of $41 000.

Winter takings are typically 18% below the seasonal average. [1]

\(\frac{41\,000}{0.82} = \$50\,000\). [1]

Monthly data for one year give a mean of 500. January's value was 650. (a) Using only this year, find January's seasonal index. (b) Explain why seasonal indices should be averaged over several years. (c) The final seasonal index for January is 1.25. By what percentage must January figures be adjusted to remove seasonality?

(a) \(\frac{650}{500} = 1.3\). [1]

(b) One year can include irregular fluctuations; averaging over several years gives a more reliable index. [1]

(c) Divide by 1.25, i.e. multiply by 0.8: decrease by 20%. [1]

Summary

  • The season is typically 30% above average. They add to the number of seasons (4, 12, …).
  • Divide each value by its year's mean, then average those ratios for each season.
  • Deseasonalised = actual ÷ SI; actual = deseasonalised × SI.
  • Dividing by 1.25 is ×0.8, a 20% decrease. Forecast: substitute t in the deseasonalised trend line, then multiply by the season's index.
