Transition diagrams and matrices

A transition matrix \(T\) describes how a fixed population moves between states each time period. The columns are where you are now (“from”, this period) and the rows are where you go (“to”, next period).

The transition matrix for the diagram
\[ T = \begin{array}{cl} \text{this week} & \\ \begin{array}{cc} A & \quad B \end{array} & \\ \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix} & \!\!\begin{array}{l} A \\ B \end{array} \text{next week} \end{array} \]

Each column adds to 1: everyone in a state goes somewhere.

“20% of A's customers switch to B” goes in column A, row B. Students who write it in row A, column B get the transpose, and every answer after that is wrong. If your columns don't add to 1, you've built it sideways.

Generating state matrices

A state matrix \(S_n\) is a column matrix of how many are in each state after \(n\) periods.

Transition matrix recurrence relation (on the formula sheet)
\[ S_0 = \text{initial state}, \qquad S_{n+1} = T S_n \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad S_n = T^n S_0 \]

This week 500 people go to cinema A and 500 to cinema B. (a) Find \(S_1\) and \(S_3\). (b) How many of A's customers switch to B in the first week?

Multiply by \(T\) once for \(S_1\); use \(T^3 S_0\) on CAS for \(S_3\). For (b), the number moving from A to B is the A → B proportion times the number at A.

\(S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 500 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 400 + 150 \\ 100 + 350 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 550 \\ 450 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(S_3 = T^3 S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 587.5 \\ 412.5 \end{bmatrix}\).

(b) \(0.2 \times 500 = 100\).

(a) \(S_1\): 550 at A, 450 at B. \(S_3\): about 588 at A, 413 at B (round sensibly, keeping the total at 1000). (b) 100 people.

Steady state

For many transition matrices, the state matrices stop changing noticeably after enough periods. This is the equilibrium or steady state.

Week \(n\)012345…50
Cinema A500550575587.5593.8596.9…600
Cinema B500450425412.5406.3403.1…400

Find the long-term number of customers at each cinema.

Calculate \(T^n S_0\) for a large \(n\) (say 50, then 51) and check the answer has stopped changing.

\(T^{50} S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 600 \\ 400 \end{bmatrix}\) and \(T^{51} S_0\) is the same.

600 at A and 400 at B. Check: \(0.8(600) + 0.3(400) = 600\). At equilibrium, the 120 moving A → B balance the 120 moving B → A.

\(T\) is regular if some power \(T^n\) has no zero elements. A regular \(T\) always settles to the same steady state (as proportions), whatever \(S_0\) is. \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) is not regular: its powers swap back and forth, and the states flip every period instead of settling.

600 students choose lunch from the canteen (C), home (H) or the shops (S) each day, with \(T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.7 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.1 & 0.7 \end{bmatrix}\) (order C, H, S) and \(S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 300 \\ 200 \\ 100 \end{bmatrix}\). Find \(S_2\) and the long-term state.

Same method: \(T^2 S_0\), then a large power.

\(S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 230 \\ 250 \\ 120 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_2 = T^2 S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 200 \\ 268 \\ 132 \end{bmatrix}\).

\(T^{50}S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 175 \\ 275 \\ 150 \end{bmatrix}\).

Day 2: 200 canteen, 268 home, 132 shops. Long term: 175, 275 and 150.

“In the long term”, “eventually” and “equilibrium” all mean steady state. Questions sometimes ask for the first period where a state reaches a value: generate \(T^n S_0\) for \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) and stop at the first one that passes it. If the state matrix gives percentages or proportions, the steady state is in the same units.

Practice

Each month 10% of members of gym X move to gym Y and 25% of Y's members move to X; everyone else stays. With columns and rows in order X, Y, the transition matrix is

  1. \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.25 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}\)
  2. \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.25 \\ 0.1 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}\)
  3. \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0.25 \\ 0.9 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}\)
  4. \(\begin{bmatrix} 0.75 & 0.1 \\ 0.25 & 0.9 \end{bmatrix}\)

Column X: stay 0.9, move to Y 0.1. Column Y: move to X 0.25, stay 0.75. Columns add to 1.

Using that matrix with \(S_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 700 \\ 300 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_1\) is

  1. \(\begin{bmatrix} 705 \\ 295 \end{bmatrix}\)
  2. \(\begin{bmatrix} 660 \\ 340 \end{bmatrix}\)
  3. \(\begin{bmatrix} 630 \\ 225 \end{bmatrix}\)
  4. \(\begin{bmatrix} 700 \\ 300 \end{bmatrix}\)

\(0.9(700) + 0.25(300) = 705\); \(0.1(700) + 0.75(300) = 295\).

A transition matrix gives \(S_{n+1} = TS_n\). To find \(S_4\) from \(S_0\), calculate

  1. \(4TS_0\)
  2. \(T^4 + S_0\)
  3. \(S_0T^4\)
  4. \(T^4S_0\)

\(S_0\) is a column matrix, so \(T\) goes on the left: \(S_4 = T^4 S_0\). \(S_0T^4\) isn't even defined.

For the gyms, the long-term number at X (from 1000 members in total) is closest to

  1. 500
  2. 700
  3. 714
  4. 750

\(T^{50}S_0 \approx \begin{bmatrix} 714.3 \\ 285.7 \end{bmatrix}\). Check: \(0.1 \times 714.3 \approx 0.25 \times 285.7 \approx 71.4\) moving each way.

An insect population is in three stages: egg (E), juvenile (J) and adult (A). Each week, 40% of eggs hatch into juveniles and the rest stay eggs; 50% of juveniles become adults and the rest stay juveniles; adults stay adults. (a) Write \(T\) (order E, J, A). (b) Starting with 1000 eggs only, how many adults are there after 2 weeks? (c) Is \(T\) regular?

(a) \(T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.4 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). [1]

(b) \(S_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 600 \\ 400 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \(S_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 360 \\ 440 \\ 200 \end{bmatrix}\): 200 adults. [1]

(c) No: the zeros above the diagonal stay in every power (nothing ever moves back to egg or juvenile). In the long run everything becomes an adult. [1]

Summary

  • Columns are “from” (this period); rows are “to” (next period). Each column adds to 1.
  • \(S_{n+1} = TS_n\), so \(S_n = T^nS_0\).
  • Calculate \(T^nS_0\) for a large \(n\) and check it no longer changes (compare with \(n + 1\)).
  • One where some power has no zero elements; it settles to the same steady state whatever the starting state.
