The set-up

\(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\) (the claim / status quo). \(H_1\): \(\mu > \mu_0\) or \(\mu < \mu_0\) (one-tailed) or \(\mu \ne \mu_0\) (two-tailed). Choose \(H_1\) from the question's wording before looking at the data.

Test statistic and p-value
\[ z = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}, \qquad p = \Pr(\bar X \text{ at least as extreme as } \bar x \mid H_0 \text{ true}) \]

Two-tailed: \(p = 2\Pr(Z > |z|)\). If \(p < \alpha\) (usually 0.05), reject \(H_0\) in favour of \(H_1\); otherwise there's insufficient evidence to reject \(H_0\).

A factory claims its batteries last 20 h on average (\(\sigma = 3\) h). A consumer group suspects the mean is lower. A sample of 36 has \(\bar x = 19.1\) h. Test at the 5% level.

"Lower" → \(H_1: \mu < 20\). Find \(p = \Pr(\bar X \le 19.1)\) assuming \(\mu = 20\).

\(H_0: \mu = 20\), \(H_1: \mu < 20\). Under \(H_0\), \(\bar X \sim N\left(20, 0.5^2\right)\).

\(z = \frac{19.1 - 20}{0.5} = -1.8\); \(p = \Pr(Z \le -1.8) \approx 0.0359\).

\(0.0359 < 0.05\): reject \(H_0\). There is evidence at the 5% level that the mean battery life is less than 20 h.

A machine fills bags to a mean of 500 g (\(\sigma = 8\)). After servicing, a sample of 25 has mean 503.5 g. Is there evidence at the 5% level the mean has changed?

"Changed" → two-tailed. Double the tail probability.

\(z = \frac{3.5}{8/5} = 2.1875\); \(\Pr(Z > 2.1875) \approx 0.0144\).

\(p \approx 0.0287 < 0.05\): reject \(H_0\); evidence the mean fill has changed (it appears to have increased).

Critical values and errors

Alternatively, find the critical value of \(\bar x\): for \(H_1: \mu < 20\) above at 5%, reject when \(\bar x < 20 - 1.6449(0.5) = 19.18\).

Type I error: rejecting \(H_0\) when it's true. Its probability is \(\alpha\). Type II error: not rejecting \(H_0\) when it's false. Its probability depends on the true \(\mu\): \(\Pr(\text{don't reject} \mid \mu = \mu_1)\).

For the battery test (reject if \(\bar x < 19.18\), \(n = 36\), \(\sigma = 3\)), find the probability of a type II error if the true mean is actually 19 h.

Type II = fail to reject = \(\bar x \ge 19.18\), computed with the true mean 19.

\(\bar X \sim N(19, 0.5^2)\): \(\Pr(\bar X \ge 19.18) = \Pr(Z \ge 0.36)\).

\(\approx 0.36\). (The test often misses a small real difference with this \(n\).)

Never "accept \(H_0\)" or say it's "proven true": say there's insufficient evidence to reject it. And the p-value is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true.

Full marks need: both hypotheses with \(\mu\) (not \(\bar x\)), the p-value, comparison with \(\alpha\), and a conclusion in context. VCAA examiners' reports repeatedly flag hypotheses written with \(\bar x\) and missing context.

Practice

A researcher wants to test whether a new diet reduces mean cholesterol below 5.2. The hypotheses are

  1. \(H_0: \bar x = 5.2,\ H_1: \bar x < 5.2\)
  2. \(H_0: \mu = 5.2,\ H_1: \mu < 5.2\)
  3. \(H_0: \mu < 5.2,\ H_1: \mu = 5.2\)
  4. \(H_0: \mu = 5.2,\ H_1: \mu \ne 5.2\)

Hypotheses are about \(\mu\); "reduces" is one-tailed.

A test gives \(p = 0.03\). Which is correct?

  1. reject \(H_0\) at the 1% level
  2. \(H_0\) has a 3% chance of being true
  3. do not reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level
  4. reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level but not at 1%

\(0.01 < 0.03 < 0.05\).

In a two-tailed test, \(z = -1.5\). The p-value is closest to

  1. 0.134
  2. 0.067
  3. 0.933
  4. 0.866

\(2\Pr(Z < -1.5) = 2(0.0668) = 0.134\).

A type I error occurs when

  1. \(H_0\) is false and is not rejected
  2. the sample is biased
  3. \(H_0\) is true but is rejected
  4. the p-value exceeds \(\alpha\)

Type I = false alarm; its probability is the significance level.

\(H_0: \mu = 70\), \(H_1: \mu > 70\), \(\sigma = 10\), \(n = 49\), \(\bar x = 72.5\). Find the p-value and conclude at 5%.

\(\operatorname{sd}(\bar X) = \frac{10}{7}\); \(z = \frac{2.5}{10/7} = 1.75\). [1]

\(p = \Pr(Z > 1.75) \approx 0.0401\). [1]

\(p < 0.05\): reject \(H_0\); evidence that \(\mu > 70\). [1]

For \(H_0: \mu = 100\), \(H_1: \mu > 100\), \(\sigma = 15\), \(n = 25\), at the 5% level: find the critical value of \(\bar x\), and the probability of a type II error if \(\mu = 106\).

\(\operatorname{sd}(\bar X) = 3\). [1]

Reject if \(\bar x > 100 + 1.6449(3) = 104.93\). [1]

Type II: \(\Pr(\bar X \le 104.93 \mid \mu = 106) = \Pr(Z \le -0.356)\). [1]

\(\approx 0.361\). [1]

Summary

  • \(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\); \(H_1\) one- or two-tailed from the wording, always in terms of \(\mu\).
  • \(z = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\); two-tailed p doubles the tail.
  • \(p < \alpha\) → reject \(H_0\); otherwise insufficient evidence. Conclude in context.
  • Type I: reject a true \(H_0\) (prob \(\alpha\)). Type II: miss a false \(H_0\).
